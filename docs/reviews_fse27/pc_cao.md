## (b) The three papers

I chose three 2025–2026 works where she is **first or corresponding author, or a named co-author on a paper squarely in the lens**, and where I could read the full text (not just the abstract). All three were read in full from arXiv HTML: introduction, method/study design, full results narrative, threats/limitations, discussion, conclusion, and (for P1) the labeling-protocol and statistics appendices.

### P1. *Position: Code Benchmarks Should Prioritize Rigor, Reliability, and Reproducibility* — ICML 2026 (Position track). **Cao is first author and corresponding author.**
arXiv: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10711> (v5, 06 Jul 2026; earlier versions were titled "How Should We Build A Benchmark? Revisiting 274/672 Code-Related Benchmarks For LLMs"). ICML poster page: <https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/poster/67049>

A decade-scale (2014–2025) survey of **672 code-related benchmarks**, hand-labeled against a 55-item lifecycle checklist (**How2Bench**) spanning design → construction → evaluation → analysis → release, with each item tagged ★/★★/★★★ by importance. The headline is a *lag between awareness and practice*: the proportion of benchmarks doing the right thing improves, but the absolute number of flawed benchmarks grows, because benchmark production is exploding (e.g. 46% of benchmarks do no quality-assurance check at all; 79.8% ignore data contamination; 67.1% do not deduplicate; 82.5% ignore code coverage when shipping test suites; 66.1% run one-pass evaluation with no repetition; 38.2% do not release prompts; 14.7% are not open-source at all; only 6.3% state their experimental environment). A **human study with 49 researchers** (half of whom have built a code benchmark) validates the checklist — all 49 agreed a checklist is needed, 47/55 items were rated important by >80% — and simultaneously exposes an awareness gap (>40% think recording hardware/software environment is unimportant). A distinctive section, **"Alternative Views"**, pre-empts three misreadings of the paper (it is not a survey; it is not an attack on specific benchmarks; it is not a demand for perfectionism), and §7.3 publishes the full manual-labeling protocol: two independent annotators, recorded textual evidence per "present" label, conflict escalation to two more annotators, consensus-only labels, **Cohen's κ 0.76–0.92**, plus the exact keyword criteria used for each attribute.

### P2. *Can Emulating Semantic Translation Help LLMs with Code Translation? A Study Based on Pseudocode* — TOSEM 2026. **Cao is a co-author; Shing-Chi Cheung is corresponding; first author Songqiang Chen.** (Her homepage lists her as corresponding author on the TOSEM version; the arXiv v1 lists Cheung.)
arXiv: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.00920> (v1, 01 Oct 2025; arXiv title "On Effective Semantic Translation for Code: A Study Based on Pseudocode") · DOI: <https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3790101>

A pure **empirical study** (no new tool) of whether routing LLM code translation through pseudocode — emulating human "semantic translation" — beats one-step direct code-to-code translation. Five strategies (direct 𝒟; via-pseudocode 𝒫; via-pseudocode-with-source 𝒫𝒞; and two hybrids that split the 10-attempt budget 5/5) × 5 LLMs (Qwen2.5-Coder-32B/7B, Phi-4, GPT-4o-mini, DeepSeek-Coder-V2-Lite) × 30 source→target PL pairs over 323 LiveCodeBench LeetCode problems = **9,690 translation tasks per LLM**, scored by execution-based pass@10, temperature 0.2, **three repetitions averaged**, with the full environment (RTX 4090s, Threadripper 3995WX, Ubuntu 22.04) stated and an artifact released. The honest headline is *complementarity, not victory*: pseudocode alone is usually **worse** than direct translation (−0.84% to −13.23% per model), but hybrids gain +4.10 / +7.44 / **+13.75 %** pass@10 on easy/medium/hard problems, with the biggest wins translating flexible→rigid PLs and into low-resource **Rust**. A dedicated contamination check re-runs everything on the 114 problems released after the *latest* model knowledge cutoff (2024-06-01) and shows the same trends. Case studies name three concrete advantages (disentangling understanding from generation; abstracting away PL-specific features like Go-vs-Rust closure capture; describing PL-unique APIs like Python `bit_count` functionally) and three concrete limitations (pseudocode that is incorrect, incomplete, or ambiguous — e.g. "to"/"downto" loop bounds).

### P3. *SWE-ABS: Adversarial Benchmark Strengthening Exposes Inflated Success Rates on Test-based Benchmark* — ICML 2026. **Cao is a co-author** (first author Boxi Yu, Limerick/Lero; corresponding Pinjia He; last author Lionel Briand).
arXiv: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00520> (v1, 28 Feb 2026) · Artifact: <https://github.com/OpenAgentEval/SWE-ABS>

A direct attack on the credibility of the SWE-Bench Verified leaderboard. Two-stage pipeline: (I) coverage-driven test augmentation using Tree-sitter program slicing to find patch-relevant but untested regions, with an explicit **test-decoupling step** so generated tests do not encode gold-patch-specific behavior; (II) mutation-driven adversarial strengthening that synthesizes plausible-but-wrong mutant patches which survive the existing suite, then generates tests to kill them. Result: **50.2% of the 500 Verified instances are strengthened** (25.1× UTBoost's 2%), **2,184 of 11,041 previously passing patches (19.78%) are rejected**, the top agent falls **78.80% → 62.20%** and from 1st to 5th, and Spearman ρ between old and new leaderboards drops 0.98 → 0.82. The paper holds itself to its own standard: it **manually inspects all 500 instances** for over-fitting tests, finds 53, reports them as a **10.6% false-negative rate**, reports results both with those instances excluded (SWE-ABS*) and after correction, hand-classifies 100 rejected patches into error types by two independent annotators (47% logic errors, 35% incomplete fixes), ablates Stage I vs Stage II (190 → 198 → 251 strengthened), generalizes to a second benchmark (SWE-Bench Pro, 150 sampled multi-language instances) and to a second base model (GLM-4.7 vs GPT-5, all else held fixed), reports **cost per strengthened instance ($4.98 vs UTBoost's $80.00)**, and closes with three named limitations (overfitting risk, intraprocedural-only slicing, gold-patch dependency).

## (c) Writing taste

Everything below is **observed** in the three papers unless marked.

- **Contribution framing is normative and consequence-first.** She does not open with "we propose X." She opens with a number that should worry you — "one in five 'solved' patches are semantically incorrect"; "in 2025 alone, the number of benchmarks that ignore code coverage nearly matches the previous ten years combined" — and only then introduces the artifact. The artifact (How2Bench, SWE-ABS) is positioned as *operationalizing* a position, not as the point.
- **Evidence means execution or human consensus; nothing else counts.** Across all three papers the accepted currencies are (i) execution-based metrics (pass@10 on real tests, patch-kill counts), (ii) large-N hand labeling with a published protocol and an inter-annotator statistic, and (iii) surveys of real researchers. Text-similarity metrics are explicitly rejected in P2 ("compared with text-based metrics like BLEU and Exact Match, the execution-based pass@10 metric can measure semantic equivalence"). LLM-as-judge appears in P1 only as a *labeled category of other people's practice*, never as her own oracle.
- **Extremely high quantification density, always with the denominator.** Percentages are written as `19.78% (2,184/11,041)`, `50.2% (251/500)`, `10.6% (53/500)`, `71.50% (409/672)`. Bare percentages essentially never appear. Deltas are given in *percentage points* and labeled as such when they are pp rather than relative.
- **Baselines are named, current, and beaten on their own metric — but compared fairly.** SWE-ABS compares to UTBoost on identical instances and reports UTBoost's cost as well as its score; P2 compares against the standard direct-translation prompt taken from prior work, plus a *transitive-via-real-PL* baseline from Macedo et al. rather than only a strawman. P1 compares How2Bench to BetterBench and states precisely on which axes it differs (scope, lifecycle division, objective, 24 vs 572 subjects).
- **Negative and unflattering results are reported in the main text, not buried.** P2's core honest finding is that its own proposed strategy *loses* to the baseline in isolation (up to −13.23%) and only wins in hybrid; that table is in the paper, not an appendix. P3 reports its own 10.6% false-negative rate and publishes a version of the headline table with the contaminated instances removed. P1's Stage-I-only ablation gives a "modest" gain and says so.
- **Threats to validity are short, specific, and each paired with a concrete mitigation *that was actually run*.** P2's three threats are subject representativeness, **data contamination** (mitigated by re-running on a post-cutoff subset and showing a whole extra table), and strategy representativeness. This is the tell: for her, a threat paragraph that only *argues* is worth less than one that *measures*.
- **Contamination is treated as a first-class design constraint, not a caveat.** P2 picks LiveCodeBench specifically because its problems postdate the old translation benchmarks, then still runs the post-cutoff-subset check. P3 leans on SWE-Bench Pro partly because it is contamination-resistant. P1 makes "did the benchmark handle contamination?" one of its labeled attributes (79.8% did not).
- **Reproducibility details are stated in-line, not gestured at.** Exact model versions and knowledge-cutoff dates in a table; quantization noted (GPTQ-Int4) with a justification; temperature, max output tokens, number of repetitions, OS, CPU and GPU; "all prompt templates in Appendix A.1"; "all hyperparameters in Appendix A.3". P1's whole §7.3 exists to make a *manual labeling* process reproducible.
- **Cost is reported.** P3 gives dollars per strengthened instance for both its method and the baseline. P1 notes that the community's shift to evaluating more LLMs "introduces substantially higher computational and financial costs, highlighting the trade-off between evaluation rigor and resource efficiency," and records that human-study participants asked for **time/monetary cost of benchmark construction** to be added to the checklist.
- **RQs are one sentence each, followed by a short paragraph of motivation.** P2 has 4 RQs, P3 has 4 RQs, each phrased as a question with an explicit "this RQ aims to…". Findings are boxed/named (`Pro-1`, `Limit-2`) so they can be cited individually.
- **Running examples carry real code, minimally elided, with author-added comments flagged as author-added.** P2 shows source/pseudocode/two translations side by side for a Python→JavaScript, Go→Rust, and Python→Go case, with a footnote saying the explanatory comments were added by the authors and not generated by the LLM. P3's Figure 1 is a real Django issue (`django__django-10973`) with the exact missing `str()` conversion. **Inference:** she will expect a differential/translation paper to show the actual defect, in source form, not describe it.
- **Favored tables/figures:** small comparison tables where every row is a configuration and every column a metric, with deltas in parentheses; ablation tables with one row per stage; longitudinal per-year bar charts; and a *rank-change* column when leaderboards are involved. She explicitly criticizes figures whose numbers cannot be read off (P1 §4.4 calls out CruxEval's figure by name).
- **Tone: hedged in the claim, bold in the framing.** The sentences that make the noise are factual counts ("we reject 2,184 of 11,041"); the sentences that make claims about mechanism are softened ("suggesting", "may not reflect", "we consider the results should be generalizable"). She writes conclusions as *positions* to argue with, and even supplies the counter-arguments herself (P1 §6 "Alternative Views", §7.1 "Trade-offs Between Benchmark Rigor and Development Efficiency" which concedes that rigor costs real time).
- **Artifacts:** released in all three cases, and in P1 the *checklist itself* is shipped in a printable appendix. Open-sourcing is not merely done; it is argued for as an obligation.
- **One blemish worth noting for honesty's sake:** P1's subject count is internally inconsistent — the abstract, intro and several sections say **672** benchmarks, while §2.2, §4.2 and Appendix C say **572**. This is almost certainly a stale number left from the earlier "274/572" versions of the paper. **Inference:** she is unlikely to forgive the same slip in a submission she reviews; consistency of the headline N across abstract/intro/tables is exactly the sort of thing her own checklist polices.

## (d) Likely reviewing standards — a concrete checklist for a paper in my lens

Items 1–7 are **strongly grounded** (they are literally How2Bench criteria, or practices she executes in her own work). Items 8–12 are **reasoned inferences** from the same evidence.

1. **State the capability and the application scenario the evaluation targets, and show every subject falls inside it.** P1 penalizes the 20% of benchmarks that never say what capability they measure and the 23.3% that never say what scenario they target; it singles out an MBPP item that tests trivia (dog-years conversion) rather than the claimed "entry-level programming" capability. Expect: "what exactly does your 34.4% coverage number measure the capability of?"
2. **Contamination must be addressed with a measurement, not a sentence.** The accepted form in her own work is: pick post-cutoff subjects, *then* re-run the headline result on the subset that postdates every studied model's knowledge cutoff and show the trend holds. State each model's cutoff date in a table.
3. **The oracle/test suite must be shown to be discriminative, not merely present.** P3's entire thesis is that passing tests is a *necessary, not sufficient* condition, and P1 flags that 82.5% of benchmarks ignore code coverage when shipping test suites. Expect a demand for coverage numbers over the code your oracle is supposed to judge, and for evidence that your oracle rejects plausible-but-wrong artifacts — ideally via mutants or seeded defects.
4. **Report the false-positive/false-negative rate of your own detector, measured by hand.** She does this at n=500 (P3's 10.6%) and n=100 (the rejected-patch taxonomy). A differential-testing paper that reports only found-defect counts without a measured misclassification rate will be marked down.
5. **Repeat the experiment; one-pass numbers are a defect.** 66.1% one-pass evaluation is one of P1's headline complaints, and P2 averages three repetitions at a stated temperature. Expect: number of repetitions, variance or at least averaged results, and a temperature/seed statement.
6. **Full reproducibility payload in the paper or a released artifact:** exact model versions and cutoffs, prompts verbatim, hyperparameters (temperature, max tokens, top_p), hardware/OS, quantization if any, logs. P1 shames the 38.2% who withhold prompts, the 50% who withhold hyperparameters, the 93.7% who withhold the environment, and the 83.3% who withhold logs.
7. **Open-source the artifact, with a license.** 14.7% not open-source and 19.3% without a license are called out as legal/ethical failures, not just inconveniences.
8. **Report time and money.** *(Inference, but well-supported: P3 reports $/instance and P1's own human-study participants asked for cost to be added to the checklist.)* For an LLM-pipeline paper: tokens or dollars per subject, and total campaign cost; for a fuzzing paper: CPU-hours and the budget each arm received.
9. **Baselines must be current, run by you, and compared on a metric the baseline's authors would accept** — plus a cost-normalized comparison when the methods have very different budgets. *(Inference from the UTBoost comparison, which reports both score and cost.)*
10. **Show the defect, in code.** *(Inference from the running-example habit across P2 and P3.)* A claim that translation X is wrong should come with the source fragment, the translated fragment, and the input that separates them.
11. **Say where your method does *not* work, in the main text.** *(Inference, strongly supported: P2's own approach loses in isolation and that table is in the body; P3 lists three limitations by name; P1 devotes a section to arguing against itself.)* A paper with no negative result anywhere will read to her as under-analyzed rather than strong.
12. **Human labeling needs a published protocol and an agreement statistic.** *(Partly observed, partly inference: P1 §7.3 gives independent annotation, evidence recording, conflict escalation, consensus-only labels, Cohen's κ range, and per-attribute keyword criteria; P3 uses two independent annotators with disagreement resolved by discussion.)* Expect κ or equivalent, and expect her to ask what the labeling rubric actually said.

## (e) Red flags that would make this reviewer reject

- **A leaderboard-style headline number with a weak or unexamined oracle.** This is the exact thing P3 was written to destroy. "Our method solves 78.8%" with no evidence the tests discriminate is, to her, not a result.
- **Contamination hand-waved.** "The models' training data may include our subjects; we leave this to future work" — P1 labels 79.8% of the field this way, as a defect, not a caveat.
- **One-pass, one-seed, one-temperature LLM results** presented as measurements.
- **Missing prompts / hyperparameters / model versions**, or "GPT-4" without a dated version, making the pipeline unreproducible.
- **Non-executable or similarity-based oracles** (BLEU, exact match, embedding similarity, un-validated LLM-as-judge) standing in for semantic equivalence when execution was possible.
- **Percentages without denominators**, or numbers that disagree between abstract, text and tables. (She would apply this to her own P1's 672/572 slip.)
- **No artifact, a dead link, a partial release, or no license.**
- **Overfitting to a gold reference presented as correctness.** P3's test-decoupling stage exists precisely because tests that encode the reference implementation's incidentals are a *bug*. A differential harness that calls every deviation from the reference a defect, without checking that the deviation is not a legitimate alternative, will be attacked on this ground.
- **A claim of novelty against stale baselines**, or a "first to do X" claim that her survey instinct will immediately test against the literature (P1 exists because she counted 672 papers by hand).
- **Threats-to-validity sections that only argue.** Three generic paragraphs with no mitigation that was actually executed reads, in her own papers' idiom, as a missing experiment.
- **A benchmark or dataset contribution that would fail her own How2Bench ★★★ items** — no dedup, no quality assurance, no coverage, no logs.

## (f) What would delight her

- **A negative or deflationary result carried by execution evidence.** Her own best work is "the number everyone believes is inflated, and here is the measurement." A paper showing that published C→Rust translations are semantically wrong on real inputs — with the C and Rust fragments and the separating input printed — is precisely her genre.
- **An oracle whose discriminative power is itself measured** (mutation score, seeded-defect recall, coverage over the judged code), rather than assumed.
- **A soundness gate that is executed, not asserted** — e.g. adjudicating with ASan + full UBSan so that "the C side was well-defined" is a measured property of each reported divergence. This maps directly onto her "necessary but not sufficient" critique of test-passing.
- **Honest complementarity claims.** "Our technique loses to the baseline alone but the union is +13.75%" is, in P2, the *result* — not an embarrassment. A paper that reports where its technique is dominated and why will earn credit rather than lose it.
- **Cost-aware reporting**: dollars/CPU-hours per finding, and a cost-normalized comparison to the baseline.
- **Cross-cutting generalization done cheaply and honestly**: a second benchmark, a second base model with everything else held fixed, a post-cutoff subset — each a small extra table, each pre-empting a reviewer question.
- **Rust as a target language.** Both P1 (the Rust-benchmark surge: 57 new Rust benchmarks in 2024–25) and P2 (pseudocode helps most for "low-resource Rust") show she is actively tracking the C/C++→Rust space; P3's future work explicitly names extending to Rust. A rigorous Rust-translation evaluation lands in a gap she has publicly flagged.
- **A shipped, licensed, documented artifact with logs** — and a paper that says what the artifact does *not* contain.
- **Self-criticism built into the paper's structure** (an "alternative views" / "why our result might be wrong" section). She wrote one into an ICML position paper; she will notice its absence.
